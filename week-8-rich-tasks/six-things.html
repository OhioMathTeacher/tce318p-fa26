What Makes a Task Rich

The six things from the Rich Task assignment

1 · Worthwhile mathematics

It targets a significant concept (your standard), not a trick or a one-step skill.

Example
Not yet: How many months have 28 days?
Richer: Find all the ways to make 10 with two numbers. How do you know you have them all?

2 · Open to learners (low floor, high ceiling)

Students can get in, and it still holds real depth.

Example
Not yet: What is 6 × 4?
Richer: A rectangle is made of 24 squares. What could it look like? How many different ones can you make?

3 · Open to strategies

It can be solved in genuinely different ways, with different representations (a diagram, a table, a number line, symbols).

Example
Not yet: Use the standard algorithm to solve 52 − 27.
Richer: Kim has 52 stickers. Lee has 27. How many more does Kim have?

4 · Demands reasoning

It asks students to show or justify their thinking, not just give an answer, so how they think becomes visible.

Example
Not yet: Which is bigger, 3/4 or 2/3? Circle one.
Richer: Which is bigger, 3/4 or 2/3? Convince a friend who thinks they are the same.

5 · Cognitively demanding

It can't be answered by a memorized procedure; it makes students actually do mathematics.

Example
Not yet: Round 347 to the nearest ten.
Richer: I rounded a number to the nearest ten and got 350. What could my number have been? Find them all.

6 · Extendable

There is a "what if…?"; it can be pushed further.

Example
Not yet: Find the perimeter of a 3 by 5 rectangle.
Richer: Then: what if the perimeter has to stay 16? What other rectangles could you make? What if it were 20?
The test of richness: if your task cannot be solved in at least three genuinely different ways, it is not rich yet. Keep building.
