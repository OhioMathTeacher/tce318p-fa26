Act II: Questions and Answers

TCE 318P · Weeks 7–13 · the research question, the lesson, and the field

You build a research question, design a lesson to investigate it, teach what your placement allows, and report what you found. Most worries about this project turn out to be about the placement rather than about the work, so start here.

What this project actually requires of you

My obligation is to make sure you engage with young learners around the teaching and learning of PK–5 mathematics. That is the requirement, and everything further down this page is a detail of it. A math block in a math classroom is one way to meet it. It is not the only way.

Concretely, this project can be done with as few as two children, and it does not have to happen in your classroom. Two or three focus students, three to five samples of their work across them, and one piece of your lesson taught to at least one of them. A small group in the hallway or the library is this project.

And no, you are not penalized for your placement. Your lesson is graded on its design, not on how much of it a classroom let you run.

Teach as much as your placement allows — one student or a small group, one session or several. At a minimum, teach one piece and collect one student's work. Your lesson-design grade does not depend on what the placement let you do — it was graded on the design.Teaching Your Lesson in the Field, §3

Before you ask your host teacher for anything

You are a guest in somebody's classroom, and you never have to win an argument to finish this project. Nothing here entitles you to a minute of her instructional time. Ask for small things, take yes for an answer, and if an ask turns awkward, drop it and fall back to two children in the hallway. That path always exists and it costs you nothing in the grade.

If a host teacher says no to everything, that is my problem to solve, not yours to argue. Tell me.

When the placement looks like a problem

My host teacher doesn't know what she'll be teaching in a month.

She doesn't have to. You are not teaching her unit. You are finding out how a student understands a concept, and a student's understanding doesn't expire when the unit ends. A child who is shaky on equivalent fractions in October is still shaky in November when the class has moved on to measurement. The concept travels with the student, not with the pacing guide.

So you do not need her long-range plan. Ask the question that needs no calendar: which group has you worried? Teachers can answer that one in a sentence, and it points you at children rather than at a unit.

And if she cannot answer even that, you still have everything you need: two children, fifteen minutes, a table in the hallway.

My teacher uses a scripted curriculum. How am I supposed to plan my own lesson?

Three ways, and the first costs almost nothing to ask for.

  • Ask for the edges, not the middle. The scripted block is the part she is accountable for. Ask whether you could add a five-minute routine before it or a three-minute exit slip after it. That is a small request and it leaves her lesson alone. You may teach the Before, the During, and/or the After, so a routine plus an exit slip is a complete instrument on its own.
  • Ask about the program's own extras. Eureka, Bridges and Illustrative Mathematics carry extension problems and "if students struggle" sidebars that nobody has time to use. Asking to try one with a few students is asking to use her curriculum, not to replace it.
  • Or step outside it entirely. Two children at a table in the hallway or the library, for fifteen minutes, needs no curricular permission at all. This is the one that always works, so reach for it the moment the other two feel like a negotiation.

Notice that none of these requires her to deviate from the program. If you find yourself explaining why your project should outrank her curriculum, stop, and take the third option.

My teacher won't give me any instructional time at all.

Then you do not use instructional time. Ask instead for two children for fifteen minutes, somewhere that is not her lesson: the hallway, the library, a table during independent work. That is a different request, and teachers who guard their teaching time often say yes to it without hesitating.

Working with one or two children is a full version of this project rather than a consolation prize. For a question about how a student understands something, a child thinking out loud over a task is better evidence than a class set of worksheets.

If even that is refused, tell me now rather than in November. A placement that will not allow one conversation with one student is a problem I need to solve with you, and October is when it is still easy.

You also will not be negotiating alone the whole time: I visit you in person at your placement during Weeks 11–12.

My placement fell through, or I came back with no student work.

Say so in class, catch me afterward, or email me. This is the single most fixable problem on this page and the one that gets worse fastest by being left alone. Everything in Weeks 7 through 13 is built on a concept and some collected work, so we sort it out before the question, not after.

I'm in an English classroom all day. How can I do a math teaching project?

You do not need a math class. You need young learners and PK–5 mathematics, and both are available to you in a room that teaches English all day.

Two or three children pulled for a small group in the hallway or the library is this project. A task interview with one child during independent reading, intervention, study hall or advisory is this project. None of that takes anybody's ELA block.

Two moves usually open it up. Ask your host teacher who else in the building you could borrow two or three students from: the grade-level math teacher, the interventionist, the resource teacher. A request that small is usually granted. And ask when the room has small-group or independent time, because that is when pulling a few students costs the teacher nothing.

If neither works, come see me. A math methods project inside an all-ELA placement is something I sort out case by case, and I would far rather do that in October than hear about it in November.

What you actually have to teach

Do I have to teach the whole lesson?

No, and the handout says so twice.

Teach one or more of your lesson's pieces — Before, During, and/or After — with one or more students. You do not have to teach the whole lesson.Teaching Your Lesson in the Field, §2

The lesson is your instrument for finding out how students understand your concept. You design the whole instrument. You run as much of it as you can.

Do I have to teach a whole class?

No. One student or a small group counts, and one session counts. The minimum is one piece of the lesson and one student's work.

Does this have to happen in the classroom?

No. Nothing in this project is confined to your classroom. A small group in the hallway, a table in the library, intervention time, a pull-out during independent work: all of it counts, and for a question about how children reason, a quiet corner is usually better than a full room.

Arrange it with your host teacher, follow the building's rules about where you may be with students, and use pseudonyms in everything you write down.

What if the student whose work I collected is absent?

Work with a backup student, someone you have already identified who is working on the same concept, and collect evidence that speaks to your question. Identify that backup before you need one.

Your concept and your question

What if the mathematics my teacher is doing isn't my concept?

That is the normal case, not a problem. A child's understanding of fractions does not switch off because the class has moved on to measurement, and your question is about the understanding, not about the unit.

So you are not asking her to change what she is teaching. You are asking for fifteen minutes with two children, and that time does not have to come out of her mathematics block or happen in her room. Ask when her students have small-group or independent time, or ask for a slot in the hallway or the library.

If the answer is no, do not make a case for it. Tell me instead.

How few children can I do this with?

Two. The Concept Profile asks for two or three focus students, and three to five work samples across them, so two children producing two or three pieces of work between them satisfies it. The real constraint is the number of samples, not the number of children.

At the teaching stage the floor is lower still: one piece of your lesson, with one student's work. So two children carries you from the profile all the way through the field.

Do not aim at the floor, though. Your Final Report needs at least three telling pieces of student work and talk, read closely, and you will not know which pieces turn out to be telling until you are writing. Two children is permitted. Two children and one worksheet is not a plan. Collect more than you think you need, every time you are in the room.

I only brought one piece of student work.

You need more. The Collected work criterion asks for three to five legible samples that actually reveal thinking about the concept. This is the one requirement on this page you cannot satisfy from your desk, because your host teacher is the only source. Ask this week.

Can I change my concept?

Yes, but talk to me rather than deciding silently, and do it now. The concept is the raw material for the question, the lesson, the field work and the final report, so a change in Week 7 is cheap and a change in Week 11 is not.

Partners, and the planning partner

Can I work with a partner?

You can work as a research pair, and you will run the question storm alongside classmates either way. But there is one question per project: your own concept, your own inquiry. You are not dividing a question in half.

Is the AI going to write my research question?

No, and if it tries, that is your cue to push back. Its job is to widen; yours is to narrow. You produce the questions, and you decide which one is worth a semester.

What gets graded is the dialogue, not the output: two or three moments from the exchange that genuinely moved your question forward, described in your own words, with what each one contributed. A one-shot exchange does not earn those points.

What you hand in

What exactly am I submitting this week?

Two documents, both in Canvas.

  • Document 1 — the Studio handoff PDF: your concept, your collected work, and your question storm.
  • Document 2 — your sharpening record: the exchanges that moved your question forward, the question you landed on, and why that one.

Document 2 does not exist until you have had the exchange, so you cannot write it in advance.

Can I use my students' real names?

Never. Pseudonyms only, with names and identifying details removed from any work before it leaves the classroom. Nothing you submit should identify a real child, and nothing the planning partner reads has a real name in it. The Studio redacts the work in your browser and scans for names before anything is exported.
