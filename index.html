About this course

TCE 318P is a methods course for preservice PK–5 teachers, focused on teaching mathematics for understanding. You will design, enact, and revise lessons that are responsive to students' developmental needs, mathematical thinking, and lived experiences — learning to read curriculum materials, assess student work, and reflect on practice through field-based experience.

The arc of the semester. The whole course builds toward one act of evidence-based inquiry: you develop a question about how students understand a mathematical concept, build a lesson to investigate it, take it into a real classroom, and report honestly on what you found.
  • Act I — Foundations (Wks 1–5). The core moves: reasoning, listening and questioning, geometry and van Hiele, differentiation, and assessment.
  • Act II — Build the investigation (Wks 6–10). A concept, a research question, and a nested Before–During–After lesson designed to reveal student thinking.
  • Act III — Field & report (Wks 11–15). Enact the lesson, collect student work, and communicate what you learned in a final report.

Course objectives

By the end of this course, candidates will be able to:

  • Design and adapt lesson plans that align with standards, address developmental progressions, and respond to student thinking.
  • Implement effective teaching practices, including questioning, active listening, and facilitation of rich mathematical tasks.
  • Analyze and assess student work to inform instructional decisions.
  • Use observation and field experience to plan targeted instruction.
  • Integrate principles of access, inclusion, and differentiation into mathematics instruction.
  • Engage in reflective practice to improve teaching and communicate professional insights.

Working with AI

On your own work, generative AI is your choice, not a requirement. When you do use it, use it as a thinking partner, not an answer generator — the reasoning has to be yours.

  • We recommend Gemini, since you all have Miami Pro accounts. Any tool is fine; none is required.
  • Human first. A person reads your work before a machine does. We practice this all semester — you test your own thinking, and a colleague reads your draft, before an AI ever weighs in.
  • Disclose it. Say how you used AI in your work (a sentence is enough), and never present AI-invented facts or citations as real.
  • Todd-in-a-Can is our course AI reviewer, built for the final paper. Run it as often as it helps; a human peer reads your draft too.

Weeks 7–10 are the exception: there, one AI tool is part of the assignment. Those four weeks use Alkimi, and each assignment tells you exactly what to bring it and what to ask.

  • What it is. A set of critics I configure against each assignment's rubric — one for your research question, one for your rich task, one for your routine, one for your whole investigation plan. You will get an invitation before Week 7; there is nothing for you to buy.
  • A critic, not a designer. It will not write your question, task, routine, or lesson, and it is built to refuse if you ask. If it ever hands you a finished product, push back — the design judgment is what's being assessed.
  • Human first, again. Your colleagues test your work before Alkimi sees it. That order is not a suggestion: the "two reads" criterion on each rubric asks what the human read surfaced, then what the machine added, and what you rejected from both.
  • Not a stand-in for children. Any strategy it names is a possibility to watch for, never a fact about how a real child thinks.
  • Pseudonyms only. Everything you put into Alkimi is de-identified first. No student names, schools, or identifying details — ever, in any tool.

Late work

Anything may be up to 48 hours late. No penalty, no need to ask, and no explanation required. Submit it and move on.

If you need longer than that, email me. You will get it. I would rather read your real work a week late than a panicked version on time. There is nothing to prove and no documentation to produce.

Three things cannot slide, because someone other than you depends on them.

  • Drafts that go to peer feedback or a lesson-planning session. A colleague has to read it, so it has to exist.
  • The Investigation Plan, Week 10. You cannot walk into a room of real children without a plan, and the field placement is not ours to reschedule.
  • The Final Report and Review & Reflection, due Wednesday, December 9. Grades are due to the university shortly after that and I cannot hold them open.

There is nothing to keep track of here. No tokens to count, no percentage per day, no ledger.

Course tools

What you need

Any current browser works, including Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari, on macOS, Windows, Linux, or a Chromebook. There is nothing to install and no account to create.

We recommend writing on a laptop or desktop. You turn in the Reading Journal by printing it to PDF from your browser, and phones and tablets handle printing inconsistently.

Journaler also works on an iPad. We recommend testing Journaler early and getting in the habit of using it on the same device each week.

The apps

Small, self-contained apps we use through the semester. Most run in any browser and record your work so you can replay your own reasoning.

  • Journaler — our reading-and-writing app: read closely, make margin notes, and keep your dated weekly Reading Journal (Act I).
  • Research Question Studio — shape your research question from your concept and student work (Week 7).
  • Metacognition suite — Metadoku, Metaplete, Metagons: puzzles that record your solving so you can replay your own reasoning.
  • Pentomino Builder — build differentiated pentomino tasks tagged by Depth of Knowledge (Week 4).
  • nVoke — flash-and-respond "what do you see?" (Week 3).
  • Sort Lab — sort a set of pieces into groups you draw and name, then sort them a different way; six sets, in English, Spanish or Chinese (Week 3).
