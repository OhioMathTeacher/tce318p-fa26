Week 4 · First session — Studio

Measurement & Data · area · Planner · a question for every learner
Today's goals: Explore shapes and their properties through play with pentominoes — and catch the questions we ask while we play. Then sort those questions by how deep the thinking goes (DOK), and start rewriting them so there's a question for every learner.

0 · Climb the ladder (~10 min)
  • Snap cubes out. Build every shape you can from one square (a monomino), then two (dominoes), three (trominoes), four (tetrominoes). Same rule throughout: squares joined edge to edge, flips and turns count as one.
  • How many of each? Write the counts on the board as we go: 1, 1, 2, 5 … Any guesses for five squares?
  • Recorders, start now. Half the room writes down every question anyone asks from here on — mine included — word for word, one per line. Don't judge them, don't fix them, just catch them.

1 · Mess around — discover the pentominoes (~20 min)
  • A pentomino is five squares joined edge to edge; flips and turns of the same shape count as one. That's all you get told.
  • A few of you come up and build them with me from snap cubes, sketching each one on Activity Sheet 1 (Making Pieces) as it turns up. Try things. Talk out loud. How do you know when you have them all?
  • Recorders keep going. Everyone else: ask about the pentominoes as we go. Anything you wonder is fair game — including whether the guess from the ladder was right.

2 · What did we ask? (~20 min)
  • Why we're doing this. Shapes and their properties — area, perimeter, symmetry — explored through play. That's van Hiele from last week (learners reason about shapes at the level they're at) meeting Week 1 (play is a way in, not a break from the math).
  • The uncomfortable finding. Left to our own devices, teachers mostly ask doing questions — what is, how many, which one — and rarely thinking questions — how do you know, what if, why can't. Sorting questions by cognitive demand helps the teacher see the ceiling of their own questioning, and helps students see the depth they're being invited to think at.
  • Open the DOK Wheel. Tap each wedge once so we've all seen what the four levels ask for.
  • Now sort what we recorded. Put every question from the mess-around into one of the four piles — best we can. Count the piles. Where did most of them land?
  • Spin. Take one of our recorded questions and rewrite it at the level the wheel lands on. What did you have to change?

3 · Double your table's pentomino (~20 min)
  • Each table gets a different pentomino — T L Z I P M. On Activity Sheet 2 (Enlarged Pentominoes), double yours using four distinct pentominoes.
  • Can you do it? If not, why not?
  • One person at each table is the recorder again — catch the questions as they happen. This time, knowing the wheel, try to ask at least one question from each of the four wedges.

4 · Come back together (~10 min)
  • Debrief. Could you double your pentomino with four distinct pieces? Which shapes couldn't — and why not?
  • Compare the piles. Where did your table's questions land this time versus the mess-around? Did knowing the wheel change what you asked?
  • Name it. Asking about the same shapes across a range of depths is differentiation — a question for every learner. Two dials: depth (DOK) and size (which shape you hand which learner). Next class you'll write questions for grade 2–4 students on both dials in the Pentomino Builder.

If you finish early
  • Two pentominoes can't be doubled with four distinct pieces. Find them — and convince us why not. (Which wedge is that question in?)
Wrap-up & looking ahead:

We'll debrief both next class. Finish doubling your pentomino too, if you ran out of time.
