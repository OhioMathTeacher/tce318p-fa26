The van Hiele levels

TCE 318P · Week 3 · Geometry & van Hiele — a reference sheet from Todd, to read alongside Higgins
This page is not part of Higgins's article. It is our own summary, added for this course. The levels are van Hiele's; the wording here is ours. Sources are listed at the bottom.
LevelWhat the learner is doingWhat it sounds like — from Higgins's second graders
1
Visual		 Describes a shape by how the whole thing looks, or by how it is turned. A square and a rectangle feel like separate, unrelated things. The name comes from the overall picture, not from any part of it. “Some shapes are long — squares are short; rectangles are long.”
2
Analysis		 Describes a shape by its properties and parts — sides, corners, straight or curved, open or closed. The properties are real and used, but each class of shape is still its own separate box. “All shapes have straight lines except for circles and ovals.”
3
Abstraction		 Relates the properties to one another and orders the classes logically. One kind of shape can be a special case of another — so a square can be a rectangle without stopping being a square. “I think they should be in with the other shapes because they do not have sides but are still in the group.”

Levels 4 (Deduction) and 5 (Rigor) complete the model — formal proof, and comparing axiom systems. They are named here for completeness and are not in play in this course. Nothing a grade 2–4 learner says should be filed under them.

Three things to hold onto

  • The levels are sequential, invariant and hierarchical. A learner works through them in order and cannot skip one. Experience moves someone up, not age — an adult who has not thought about shapes since grade school can sit at Level 1.
  • Locate, don't rank. You name a level in order to offer the right next experience. A “diamond” answer is data, not an error. An experience aimed above where someone is only gets memorised.
  • The label tells you almost nothing; the justification is the diagnosis. Two learners can both say “rectangle” from completely different levels. Always ask how do you know?

A note on the numbering

You will see this ladder numbered two different ways, and both are correct. Van Hiele originally numbered the levels 0–4. American writers renumbered them 1–5, freeing up “level 0” to describe a child who does not yet recognise shapes at all. Higgins explains this on p. 493 of the article you just read. In this course we use 1–5 — so Visual is Level 1, and what some texts call “Level 0” or “informal deduction” you will find here as Levels 1 and 3.
