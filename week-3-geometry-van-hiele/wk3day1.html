Week 3 · First session — Studio

Geometry, 2.G / 3.G attributes · Poser · the same figure, seen different ways
Today's goals: Run your Excursion rationale past genAI — together, in the room — then live three What Do You See? / How Do You Know? geometry talks. Feel how one figure gets seen many ways — "diamond," "rhombus," "parallelogram" — and that the spread isn't error, it's real. We name what it is next class; today you just live it.

1 · Run your rationale past genAI — together (~20 min)

  • I hand back Pedagogical Excursion 2. Photograph your question & rationale sheet now, then give genAI the photo with this prompt:

I'm training to be a teacher. A third grader solved 83 − 18 as 8 − 3 = 5 and 8 − 1 = 7, getting 75 (subtracting the smaller digit from the larger in each column). The attached photo shows a question I would ask this student and my reasons for asking it. Please don't re-diagnose the student or rewrite my question — just react to my reasoning: is my rationale specific to this student's error, or too general? How could I make it more specific to this child's thinking?

  • Read what it pushed you on: was your reasoning specific to that child, or too general? Would you revise your question?
  • Around the room — where did genAI help, and where did it miss?

Purpose: carry the questioning stance forward — from critiquing your own rationale to reading how others see. Doing it here means we can compare what it told each of us, which is the part that teaches.

2 · Three geometry talks — you present (~60 min · ~20 each)

  • We run each routine together — flash a figure, "What do you see?", collect a few, then "How do you know?" Ask, never tell.
  • WODB Which One Doesn't Belong? — four figures; pick one that doesn't belong and defend it (every figure can be defended). Pick a set from Which One Doesn't Belong?.
  • Same & Different — two figures; say what's the same and what's different. Pick a pair from Same But Different — Geometry (28 pairs, each with a full-size view for projecting).
  • Sort — a handful of figures; group them and state your rule, then sort them a different way. Run it in Sort Lab — drag the pieces into circles you draw and name, and turn or flip them to test whether your rule survives. Start with Triangles (none of them sits on a flat base, on purpose). Then try Characters: you can't name what you can't read, so the only way in is properties. Shapes, Letters, Foods and People are there too, and the whole board runs in English, Spanish or Chinese.

Purpose: three routines keep the demand varied, but all surface the same thing — how differently people see the same form.

If you finish early

  • Take one figure we ran and find a reason to include it that no one gave. How many honestly-different "sees" can one figure hold?
