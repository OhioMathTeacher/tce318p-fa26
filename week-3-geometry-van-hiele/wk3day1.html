Week 3 · First session — Studio

Geometry, 2.G / 3.G attributes · Poser · the same figure, seen different ways
Today's goals: Feel how one figure gets seen many ways — "diamond," "rhombus," "parallelogram" — and that the spread isn't error, it's real. We name what it is next class; today you just live it.

1 · Your five questions, read back to you (~20 min)
  • I hand back Pedagogical Excursion 2. Photograph only question 2 — your five questions. Not the whole sheet: my marks are on it, and a model that can see a grade starts agreeing with the grade instead of reading your work.

I'm training to be a teacher. A third grader solved 83 − 18 as 8 − 3 = 5 and 8 − 1 = 7, getting 75 (subtracting the smaller digit from the larger in each column). The attached photo shows five questions I wrote to ask this student. Please don't re-diagnose the student, and don't write better questions for me. For each of my five, tell me whether it is genuinely open or whether it funnels toward the answer I already have in mind — and quote the words in my question that made you say so.

  • Read what it pushed you on. Which of yours actually funnel? Would you rewrite one?
  • Now finish the move: pick the one question you'd open with, and say in a sentence why it fits this child — this misconception, not a general prompt.
  • Around the room — where did genAI help, and where did it miss?

2 · WODB — Which One Doesn't Belong? (~15 min)
  • A few as a class. Four figures up; pick one that doesn't belong and defend it — every figure can be defended. "What do you see?", collect a few, then "How do you know?" Ask, never tell. Sets from Which One Doesn't Belong?
  • Aim your four at the boundary that actually splits levels — square, tilted square, rhombus, rectangle, parallelogram. A star next to a heart only gets you appearance talk.

3 · Same & Different (~15 min)
  • A few as a class. Two figures; say what's the same and what's different — then "How do you know?" Pairs from Same But Different — Geometry (28 pairs, each with a full-size view for projecting).
  • The pair that teaches is the one where the answer is obvious and the reason is not.

4 · Sort — one board per group (~15 min)
  • Each group takes a different board in Sort Lab — Triangles, Characters, Shapes, Letters, Foods, People.
  • Mess around first. Drag the pieces into circles you draw and name, state your rule, then sort a different way and turn or flip pieces to see whether the rule survives.
  • Triangles: none of them sits on a flat base, on purpose. Characters: you can't name what you can't read, so the only way in is properties.
  • Then together. Each group states its rule; the room tries to break it with a piece that should have fitted.

5 · Ten-Frame Flash and Dot Patterns (~15 min)
  • Two more routines, run as a room. The dots appear, then vanish, then — "How many did you see? How did you see it?"
  • You hide dots because the whole question is what you grabbed in that instant. You leave a figure up, as we did all hour, because its reasoning dies the moment it is gone. Same app, opposite move, for a reason.
  • Different ladder, same work: Count by ones · Subitize a group · Compose / decompose. Locating a level is a move — it is not a van Hiele trick.
  • These run in nVoke, which you are inside for the whole of next class.
Wrap-up & looking ahead:
  • Read Newell & Orton, “Classroom Routines” before our next class — it is the teacher’s-side account of the three routines you just ran. Save Higgins for after Thursday, once the levels have a name; you meet them in the room first, on purpose. Both are in the Week 3 Reading Homework.
