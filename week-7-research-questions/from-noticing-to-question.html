From Noticing to a Research Question

TCE 318P · Week 7 · Act II

A research question does not arrive finished. It starts as something you noticed in the room, becomes a first question, and then gets turned until a lesson you build could actually answer it. The middle column is not a mistake. It is where every one of these started.

What you noticedFirst questionTurned toward research
One of them understood making ten and still wrote the wrong number down. Her strategy was right and her answer was not. How can I help first graders get better at adding within 20? How do first-grade students use the make-10 strategy and ten-frames to solve addition problems within 20? Named the strategy, the tool and the range, so you know what work would answer it.
Two of them broke a 6 by 4 array into two 3 by 4s. Another built it up out of smaller pieces instead. Why are some students better at multiplication than others? How do students with different approaches to multiplication use composing and decomposing arrays to explain their mathematical thinking? Expects children to differ, and goes looking for the difference rather than ranking them.
They all had the same problems and could pick their own strategy. One of them miscounted nine groups of five. How do the students figure out the answer to the question 7 x 8? How do students work out a product they do not already know? Widened from one fact to the thinking behind any of them.
Under a three-minute timer, one of them started adding instead of multiplying. How do students gain confidence when learning new skills, and keep themselves pushing to go further? When the timer is on, what do students do with a fact they have not memorized? Asks one thing instead of two, and the mathematics is now in the sentence.
They drifted when place value was on the board. They stayed with it when the blocks were in their hands. What can teachers do to make place value feel less abstract for first graders? What do first graders do differently with place value when they have blocks in their hands? The subject moved from the teacher to the children.
She plotted 3/4 past the 1 on the number line. What do students need to know about number lines in order to correctly plot a fraction? How do students decide where a fraction goes on a number line? One word out. “Correctly” was asking whether she got it right.

Five things to read your own question against

  1. It asks one thing. Not two or three joined with “and.”
  2. It is open. No single right answer, and not yes or no.
  3. It is about their reasoning, not about whether they got it right.
  4. It names the mathematics, and it is not a verdict on a child.
  5. A lesson you could build would answer it.

Four of these five come straight out of the Concept Profile guidelines: “one specific, grade-appropriate mathematical concept” and “an open question about how students understand this concept, not whether they got it right, and not a diagnosis of a child.” The fifth is the one the rest of Act II depends on.
